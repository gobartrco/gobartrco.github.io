That polysynth sitting under a dust cover may be worth more to another producer than it is to you. A trade can turn unused gear into the drum machine, controller, or desktop synth that actually fits your next track. But learning how to trade synth gear means treating the exchange like a real transaction, not a casual DM with a stranger.

Synth trades have more moving parts than a simple cash sale. Condition can be hard to judge from photos, values move with demand, and one missing power supply or sticky key can change the deal. A good trade starts with accurate information, clear expectations, and protection that gives both people a reason to follow through.

## Start With the Real Value of Your Synth

The price you originally paid is not the trade value. Neither is the highest asking price you can find online. Start by looking at recent completed sales for the exact model, then adjust for condition, included accessories, cosmetic wear, and local demand.

A discontinued synth with a cult following may carry a premium. A current-production model that is easy to buy new may not. Be honest about which category yours falls into. If your unit has been modified, repaired, or has an intermittent issue, say so before anyone makes an offer. Hidden problems do not become less serious because the other person is offering a cool piece of gear.

Trade value is often a range, not one fixed number. If your synth commonly sells for $650 to $750, using $700 as a working value is reasonable. If the other item is worth $550, the trade might include cash on top, another small piece of gear, or a different offer altogether.

Do not confuse scarcity with value. A synth can be hard to find and still have limited demand. Likewise, a common machine in excellent condition with its original box, power supply, manual, and factory accessories may be easier to trade than a rare unit with an unknown repair history.

## Prepare a Listing That Leaves No Guesswork

A vague listing invites vague offers and frustrating conversations. Give potential trade partners enough information to decide whether the gear works for them before they contact you.

Describe the exact model and version, overall condition, [known functional issues](/articles/how-to-list-synths-for-trade), included accessories, and what you want in return. If you are open to multiple categories, say so. A producer looking for a sample-based workstation may be open to a synth plus cash, while someone chasing an analog monosynth may only want a direct swap.

Photos and video are part of the deal, not an afterthought. Show the unit powered on and working, then document the details a buyer or trader would inspect in person:

- The front panel, keys, knobs, sliders, and screen
- The rear panel, power input, MIDI, USB, audio outputs, and serial number
- Any scratches, dents, rack rash, missing caps, or discoloration
- Included power supplies, adapters, cases, manuals, and original packaging
- A short video demonstrating sound, controls, and display behavior

For synths with lots of controls, mention what you tested. Did every key trigger? Do all encoders respond normally? Does MIDI work? Are the outputs clean? You do not need to perform a factory-service inspection, but you should not claim everything works if you have only turned it on once.

## How to Trade Synth Gear With Fair Terms

A fair trade is not just about matching dollar values. It is about matching the full condition and risk on both sides. Before either person packs anything, agree in writing on the exact items, their stated condition, the value assigned to each item, who pays any cash difference, and [who covers shipping](/articles/who-pays-shipping-in-trades).

Be specific about accessories. “Includes power supply” is not enough if the synth requires a proprietary adapter, a breakout cable, or a particular external PSU. Clarify whether the original box is included, whether a stand or case is part of the trade, and whether software licenses or expansion packs can actually be transferred.

Cash added to a trade deserves the same care as the gear itself. Confirm the amount, payment method, and when it will be sent. Avoid arrangements where one person is expected to send an extra payment later based on trust alone. If the cash difference is substantial, a structured platform that records the terms and holds both sides accountable is the safer choice.

If the values are close but not identical, do not force a deal over a small gap. An extra patch cable, MIDI controller, pedal, or modest cash adjustment can make sense. But if the gap requires a pile of unrelated add-ons, the trade may be getting too complicated to be worth the risk.

## Check the Person, Not Just the Gear

A great listing does not make a great trade partner. Look for a consistent profile, clear communication, detailed photos, and a willingness to answer reasonable questions. Someone who pressures you to ship immediately, refuses to show the synth operating, or keeps changing the terms is giving you useful information. Listen to it.

Ask for current photos or a short video with a specific request, such as the synth powered on beside a handwritten date. For higher-value gear, request close-ups of the serial number and the controls most likely to have issues. This is not paranoia. It is basic verification before you hand over equipment that may be difficult to replace.

Reputation matters, especially when you cannot meet in person. Reviews, prior completed trades, and documented communication create a track record that a new social media account cannot provide. On a protected music-gear marketplace such as Bartr, deposits, [delivery confirmation](/articles/delivery-confirmation-for-gear-trades), reputation scores, and human-led dispute support add structure when two strangers are trading across the country.

## Pack a Synth Like It Has to Survive a Drop

Synthesizers are not pedals. Keybeds, displays, encoders, and power supplies can all be damaged by a box that is merely "packed pretty well." The original box and inserts are best, but only if they are still structurally sound. Put that box inside a larger outer box with padding around every side when possible.

Remove loose accessories from the synth itself. Wrap power supplies and cables separately so they cannot slam into the unit during transit. Protect knobs and sliders from pressure, and use a plastic bag or wrap to keep packing material out of vents and key gaps. For a keyboard synth, make sure the keybed cannot shift inside the box.

Take timestamped photos and video before packing, during packing, and after the box is sealed. Photograph the shipping label and keep the receipt. Use tracked shipping, signature confirmation for valuable gear, and insurance that reflects the actual declared value. Cheap shipping is not a win if it leaves both sides exposed when a carrier mishandles the box.

## Inspect Before You Confirm the Trade Is Complete

When your incoming gear arrives, document the unboxing before you throw away packing materials. Check the model, serial number, accessories, exterior condition, power behavior, keys, controls, display, audio outputs, MIDI, and any special functions the other person represented as working.

Do this promptly. Waiting a week to test a synth makes it harder to separate a shipping issue from something that happened after delivery. If there is a problem, keep communication factual: describe the issue, share photos or video, and refer back to the agreed condition. Do not start with accusations when a clear record may solve the problem faster.

Some differences are normal in used gear. A faint cosmetic mark, a slightly loose knob, or a missing generic cable may not justify blowing up a fair deal. A dead voice, cracked screen, nonfunctional keyboard, or undisclosed repair history is different. The right response depends on the issue, the evidence, and the terms you agreed to before shipping.

The best synth trade should feel exciting after it arrives, not stressful for days beforehand. Be accurate about your gear, realistic about value, deliberate about terms, and willing to walk away when the other side will not meet that standard. The right instrument will come along, and a protected trade gives you a much better chance of enjoying it when it does.
