Your amp still works. It just does not work for you anymore.

Maybe you bought a 100-watt head for a band that never got loud enough to use it. Maybe your combo is too heavy to haul to rehearsal every week. Maybe you have been chasing a different tone and the amp sitting in your corner is the budget to get there — if you can trade it for something better suited to where your playing is now.

Trading a guitar amp online makes sense. But amps are not small, cheap, or easy to ship. They are heavy, fragile in transit, and expensive enough that a bad trade stings. That combination means the process matters more than it does for a cable swap or a used pedal.

This guide walks through how to trade a guitar amp online the right way — from valuing your gear and writing a listing that attracts serious offers, to shipping safely and protecting yourself if something goes wrong.

---

## 1. Know What Your Amp Is Actually Worth

Before you list anything, you need a realistic number. Not what you paid. Not what you wish it was worth. What the market will actually support right now.

Start with completed sales on resale platforms. Search for your exact model — not just the brand — and filter for sold listings. Pay attention to condition, included accessories, and whether the sale included a footswitch, cover, or original box. Those details move the number.

Then adjust for your specific amp:

- **Condition**: Tolex tears, grill cloth stains, worn knobs, and cosmetic damage all reduce value. Functional issues — a scratchy volume pot, a noisy channel switch, a loose output jack — reduce it further.
- **Service history**: A recent retube, bias adjustment, or cap job adds value and gives buyers confidence. Document it if you have it.
- **Accessories**: Original footswitches, covers, and matching speaker cabinets are worth including in the listing and factoring into the trade value.
- **Modifications**: Some mods add value to the right buyer. Others narrow your audience. Be specific about what was done and by whom.

## 2. Write a Listing That Earns Trust

A good amp listing does two things: it attracts the right trade offers and it filters out the wrong ones. Both matter.

**Lead with the essentials**: Make, model, year (if relevant), wattage, speaker configuration, and condition. Do not make someone read three paragraphs to find out it is a 15-watt combo.

**Be specific about condition**: “Good condition” means nothing. “Light tolex wear on the corners, grill cloth is clean, all controls function normally, recently retubed with JJ EL84s” means something. Specificity signals honesty and saves time for both sides.

**List what is included**: Footswitch, cover, original box, power cable, manual. If it does not come with the amp, say so.

**Disclose known issues**: A scratchy reverb knob, a channel that cuts out occasionally, a speaker that was replaced — these are not deal-killers for the right buyer, but hiding them is a deal-breaker for everyone. Disclose early and let the trade value reflect it.

**State what you are looking for**: Amp trades work best when both sides are clear about what they want. If you are open to guitars, pedals, or studio gear, say so. If you only want another amp in a specific wattage range, say that too.

## 3. Photograph It Like You Mean It

Photos are the first thing a potential trade partner sees. They are also the record of condition before anything ships.

Shoot in natural light or a well-lit room. Avoid flash, which flattens detail and hides texture. Get the full amp from the front, back, and both sides. Then get close on:

- The control panel (all knobs, switches, and labels visible)
- The speaker grill (any tears, stains, or pushed-in areas)
- The tolex or cabinet (corners, edges, any damage)
- The back panel (inputs, outputs, speaker jacks, serial number)
- Any cosmetic damage, even if minor

If the amp has been modified, photograph the inside if you can do so safely. If it has been retubed, photograph the tubes. If it has a footswitch or cover, photograph those too.

Good photos reduce questions, build confidence, and protect you if there is a dispute about condition after delivery.

## 4. Find the Right Trade Partner

Once your listing is live, the quality of your trade depends heavily on who you are trading with. Not every offer is worth taking.

Look for trade partners who:

- Have a clear listing with photos and honest condition notes
- Respond promptly and communicate specifically (not just “interested, DM me”)
- Have a trade history or reputation score you can review
- Are willing to discuss terms openly before committing

Be cautious of offers that feel rushed, vague, or too good. An amp that is listed at twice its market value “for trade only” is usually a sign that someone is trying to extract cash value from a trade they could not get in a straight sale.

## 5. Agree on Terms Before Anyone Ships

This is where most amp trades go wrong. Both sides assume they are on the same page, and they are not.

Before anything ships, confirm in writing:

- **Exactly what each person is sending**: Model, condition, included accessories, known issues
- **Any cash adjustment**: Who pays, how much, and when
- **Who pays shipping**: Each person typically pays to ship their own item, but if costs are significantly uneven, adjust the trade terms accordingly
- **Shipping method**: Carrier, insurance amount, signature confirmation if applicable
- **What happens if something arrives damaged or not as described**: Know the process before you need it

[Who Pays Shipping in Trades?](https://bartr.co/articles/who-pays-shipping-in-trades) is worth reading before you finalize terms — especially for heavier amps where freight costs can be a real variable.

Do not ship until both sides have confirmed the terms and any deposits or platform protections are in place.

## 6. Pack Your Amp Like It Has to Survive the Worst

Amps are not easy to ship. They are heavy, the chassis can shift inside the cabinet, and tubes are fragile. A bad pack job can turn a clean trade into a dispute.

If you have the original box and foam inserts, use them. They were designed for the amp and offer the best protection.

If you do not:

- Use a double-wall cardboard box sized as close to the amp as possible
- Wrap the amp in moving blankets or thick foam, then fill remaining space with packing peanuts or crumpled paper — no air pockets
- For combo amps, remove or secure the speaker if possible to prevent it from shifting
- For tube amps, remove the tubes if they are accessible and pack them separately with clear labeling
- Seal every seam with heavy-duty tape

Photograph the packed amp before you seal the box. Photograph the sealed box. Keep your shipping receipt and tracking number. These records matter if a damage claim is needed.

## 8. Confirm Delivery Before You Release Anything

When your trade partner’s amp arrives, inspect it before you confirm delivery. Do not just check that a box showed up.

Open it carefully. Photograph the packaging before you remove the amp. Check for shipping damage — dents, cracked tolex, broken handles, damaged knobs. Power it up and test every channel, every control, every input and output. If tubes were shipped separately, reinstall them and test again.

If everything matches the listing and the agreed condition, confirm delivery through the platform. That releases the trade and closes the deal.

If something is wrong — damage that was not disclosed, missing accessories, a condition that does not match the listing — document it immediately with photos and contact the platform before confirming. Do not confirm delivery on gear you are not satisfied with.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Overvaluing your amp**: Sentimental value is real, but it does not transfer to a trade partner. Price to the market.

**Skipping the condition conversation**: Assume nothing. Ask about every detail that matters to you before agreeing to terms.

**Using informal channels for high-value trades**: A DM agreement with no platform protection is a handshake deal. It works until it does not.

**Rushing the pack job**: An amp that arrives damaged because of poor packaging is your problem, not the carrier’s, unless you insured it properly.

**Confirming delivery before you have tested the amp**: Once you confirm, the trade is done. Test first.
