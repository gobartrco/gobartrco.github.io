## What Is Reverb?

Reverb (reverb.com) launched in 2013 and quickly became the dominant online marketplace for buying and selling musical instruments and gear. Think of it as eBay, but purpose-built for musicians. You list your gear, set a price, and wait for a buyer to pay cash. Reverb handles payment processing, offers buyer protection on qualifying transactions, and takes a percentage of each sale.

It's a massive platform with millions of listings — guitars, amps, pedals, synths, drums, recording gear, you name it. If you want to sell something for cash, Reverb is genuinely excellent. The search tools are solid, the audience is huge, and the transaction infrastructure is mature.

But here's the thing: **Reverb is a cash marketplace.** Gear-for-gear trades are not a native feature. If you want to swap your Telecaster for someone's Jazzmaster, you're essentially on your own — two strangers trying to coordinate a trade through a platform that wasn't designed for it.

## What Is Bartr?

Bartr (bartr.co) is a peer-to-peer online marketplace built from the ground up for one specific purpose: trading music equipment between musicians. No cash sales. No auction listings. Just structured, protected gear-for-gear swaps.

On Bartr, you list what you have and what you're looking for. The platform matches you with other musicians who want to trade. When both parties agree on a swap, Bartr's trade protection system kicks in — deposits, delivery confirmation, structured trade terms, and a reputation system built on reviews from real completed trades. If something goes sideways, human-led dispute resolution is there to sort it out.

It's a focused platform, and that focus is exactly what makes it different.

## Reverb vs Bartr: Key Differences

### 1. Trade vs. Sell — The Core Distinction

This is the most important difference in the **reverb vs bartr music gear trading** conversation, and it shapes everything else.

Reverb is optimized for cash transactions. You can technically arrange a trade through Reverb's messaging system, but the platform offers no native support for it. There's no structured swap agreement, no mutual deposit system, no trade-specific confirmation flow. You're improvising on infrastructure built for something else entirely.

Bartr is built exclusively for trades. Every feature on the platform — from listing to matching to settlement — is designed around the gear-for-gear swap. That's not a limitation. That's the point.

### 2. Trade Protection

When you sell on Reverb, you're covered by their standard buyer/seller protection policies, which are solid for cash transactions. But when you try to trade? You're largely unprotected. If you ship your amp and the other person ghosts you, Reverb's policies weren't written with that scenario in mind.

Bartr's trade protection is purpose-built for swaps:

- **Deposits** hold both parties accountable before anything ships
- **Delivery confirmation** ensures both items are received before the trade is finalized
- **Structured trade terms** mean both musicians agree to the same conditions upfront
- **Reputation scores and reviews** let you vet who you're trading with before you commit
- **Human-led dispute resolution** means a real person steps in if something goes wrong

For anyone who's been burned by an unstructured P2P trade — and plenty of musicians have — this infrastructure isn't a nice-to-have. It's the whole reason to use a dedicated platform.

### 3. Fees

Reverb charges a selling fee (currently around 5% of the transaction, plus payment processing fees). For cash sales, that's a reasonable cost of doing business on a large marketplace.

Bartr's fee structure is designed around trades, not sales. You're not paying a percentage of a "sale price" because there is no sale price — you're swapping gear. The platform's model reflects that reality, keeping costs aligned with what you're actually doing.

### 4. Community and Audience

Reverb's audience is enormous and diverse — collectors, hobbyists, working musicians, vintage dealers, and everyone in between. That scale is great for cash sales. But it also means the platform isn't specifically tuned for the musician who wants to trade a pedalboard for a new snare kit.

Bartr's community is specifically musicians who want to trade gear. Everyone on the platform is there for the same reason you are. That shared intent changes the quality of interactions — you're not sifting through cash buyers to find the one person open to a swap.

### 5. Gear Categories

Both platforms cover the core categories: guitars, amps, pedals, synths, drums, and recording/studio gear. Reverb has a broader inventory by sheer volume. Bartr's inventory is smaller but growing — and every listing is from someone who actually wants to trade, not sell.
