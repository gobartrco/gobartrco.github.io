A guitar can look spotless in listing photos and still have a lifting bridge. A synth can power on while hiding dead keys, noisy outputs, or a failing screen. When you trade with someone you do not know, condition is not a small detail. It is the value of the deal.

Knowing **how to verify music gear condition** means looking beyond a seller’s rating of “excellent” or “works great.” You need enough evidence to understand what the gear is, what it does today, what it has been through, and whether both sides agree on its condition before anything ships.

## Start With a Clear, Shared Condition Standard

Words like “mint,” “good,” and “player’s condition” mean different things to different musicians. One person sees buckle rash as normal wear. Another sees it as a price-changing flaw. Do not trade based on vague labels alone.

Ask the other trader to describe the item in three separate ways: cosmetic condition, functional condition, and known issues. Cosmetic condition covers finish wear, scratches, dents, missing knobs, tears in tolex, rack rash, and corrosion. Functional condition covers whether every essential feature works as intended. Known issues cover anything intermittent, modified, repaired, or not tested.

A useful condition statement is specific: “All six strings ring cleanly, electronics work without cutting out, and the neck is straight. There is a chip on the lower bout and light fret wear in first position.” That gives you something real to evaluate. “Plays amazing” does not.

For a trade, both items deserve the same scrutiny. Be as transparent about your own gear as you expect the other person to be. A fair exchange starts when neither side has to guess what is arriving.

## Ask for Current Photos, Not Just Listing Photos

Photos should document the actual item at the time of the trade. Request recent, well-lit images from multiple angles, especially of areas where damage or wear tends to hide. If the photos are old, heavily filtered, dark, or cropped tightly, ask for new ones.

For most gear, you should see the front, back, sides, corners, controls, serial number area, and any included accessories. Ask for close-ups of every disclosed flaw. A scratch that sounds minor can be a finish crack, water damage, or an impact point once you see it clearly.

For higher-value items, request a photo with the trader’s name and the current date written on paper beside the gear. It is a simple way to confirm they have the item in hand and are not reusing photos from an old listing.

Do not treat a lack of photos as a minor inconvenience. If someone cannot or will not show basic proof of condition, you do not have enough information to make a confident trade.

### Check for Signs of Repairs, Mods, and Missing Parts

Repairs and modifications are not automatically bad. A professionally refretted guitar, serviced vintage amp, or pedal with a useful switch mod may be more dependable than an untouched item. The issue is undisclosed work.

Look for mismatched screws, non-original hardware, rough soldering, replaced transformers, extra drill holes, stripped fasteners, missing battery doors, and changed speaker or pickup models. Ask who performed the work, when it was done, and whether receipts or service notes are available.

Originality matters more for some gear than others. A touring player may value a reliable replacement speaker over a period-correct original. A collector may feel the opposite. Make sure the trade value reflects the actual configuration, not the model as it left the factory.

## Verify Function With a Video Demo

Photos prove appearance. Video is where you verify function.

Ask for one continuous, current video that shows the gear powering on and doing the job it is supposed to do. It does not need to be a polished demo. In fact, a straightforward phone video is often better because it is harder to edit around a problem.

For a guitar or bass, have the trader show the neck, play every fret if practical, switch through pickups, turn each knob, and plug into an amp at a usable volume. Listen for crackling pots, output cuts, fret buzz, weak pickups, and tuning instability. Ask whether the truss rod works and whether the instrument has dead spots or structural repairs.

For amps, video should show startup, channel switching, control changes, speaker output, reverb or effects, and operation at both low and moderate volume. Tube amps deserve extra care. Ask about recent service, tube changes, unusual hum, popping, loss of volume, and whether the amp has been transported safely. Never assume “it turns on” means an amp is healthy.

[For pedals](/articles/how-to-trade-guitar-pedals-online-safely), verify bypass, engaged signal, footswitches, knobs, jacks, power input, and every mode or preset. For digital pedals, request proof that the screen, encoders, MIDI, USB, expression input, and firmware-dependent features work if those functions affect the deal.

Synths, drum machines, interfaces, and studio gear need a broader test. Confirm every key, pad, encoder, fader, input, output, display, button, and power supply. For recording equipment, ask what computer, operating system, and connection type were used during testing. A working FireWire interface may not fit your current setup without additional hardware.

## Confirm Identity, Serial Numbers, and Included Items

Verify that the item is the model and version being represented. Manufacturers often make meaningful changes under the same product name: different speaker configurations, updated converters, revised power supplies, or regional voltage versions.

Ask for a readable serial number photo when one exists. Check that it is present, untampered with, and consistent with the claimed item. For sensitive or high-value gear, sharing the full serial number within the structured transaction record is smarter than posting it publicly. It creates a reference point if the wrong item arrives or ownership becomes a concern.

Then confirm exactly what comes with it. “Includes everything” is not a useful term. Put the essentials in writing: power supply, case, gig bag, footswitch, tremolo arm, mounting hardware, cables, original box, manuals, expansion cards, and any proprietary adapters. A missing power supply or rack ear can materially change what the gear is worth and whether you can use it immediately.

## Compare Condition Against Trade Value

Condition should change the value, but not every flaw deserves the same adjustment. Light finish wear on a reliable working guitar is different from a repaired headstock crack. A pedal with Velcro is different from one with intermittent switching. A vintage synth with a newly serviced power supply may be a better trade than a cleaner-looking unit with unknown maintenance.

Ask yourself two questions: Can I use this as soon as it arrives? And would I be comfortable describing it to the next musician exactly as it was described to me? If either answer is no, pause the trade or renegotiate the terms.

It also depends on your purpose. A gigging musician may accept cosmetic wear for a dependable backup amp. A producer may prioritize quiet outputs and stable drivers over an original box. A collector may need unmodified parts and documented provenance. The right condition is not universal, but the facts should be.

## Document the Final Agreement Before Shipping

Once you are satisfied, capture the agreed condition in the transaction details. Include the model, serial number where applicable, disclosed flaws, modifications, included accessories, and how the gear was tested. Keep the wording simple and specific.

Before packing, each trader should take timestamped photos and a working video of the exact item being shipped. Photograph the serial number, accessories, and the packing process, including protective materials and the sealed box. This record helps distinguish pre-existing condition from shipping damage if there is a problem later.

Use tracked shipping, require [delivery confirmation](/articles/delivery-confirmation-for-gear-trades), and do not rush to mark a trade complete before the item arrives and can be inspected. A structured marketplace such as Bartr gives both musicians a clearer record, [deposits](/articles/security-deposit-for-item-swaps), delivery confirmation, and a path to human help if the gear does not match the agreement.

### What to Do When Something Does Not Match

If the item arrives with undisclosed damage, missing parts, or a functional issue, document it immediately. Take clear photos and video before attempting repairs or modifications. Compare what you received with the pre-trade condition statement and shipping documentation, then communicate through the transaction record rather than trying to sort it out through scattered texts or social messages.

Be factual. “Output cuts out when the cable moves” is more useful than “this is broken.” If an issue may be shipping-related, keep the original packaging until the situation is resolved. Fast, clear documentation gives everyone the best chance of a fair outcome.

The goal is not to make every used piece of gear look new. It is to make sure the gear you receive is the gear you agreed to trade for. A few careful questions before shipping can protect your budget, your next session, and the trust that keeps musician-to-musician trading worth doing.
